Security asks whether a setting is safe. Governance asks two other things: how
you prove the platform beneath you was audited, and how you hold dozens of
accounts to one set of rules. Both belong to Security and Compliance.

## Proving compliance with AWS Artifact

AWS Artifact provides on-demand downloads of AWS security and compliance
documents: reports on compliance with International Organization for
Standardization (ISO) standards and Payment Card Industry (PCI) Security
Standards, and System and Organization Controls (SOC) reports. AWS provides them
free of charge. You submit them to auditors or regulators as audit artifacts:
they are the third-party attestations behind the controls you inherit from AWS
rather than implement yourself.

Artifact has a second job that questions like to test. You use it to review,
accept, and track the status of your agreements with AWS, for one account or for
your whole organization. The example AWS names is HIPAA: companies subject to it
typically require a Business Associate Addendum (BAA) with AWS so that protected
health information is appropriately safeguarded. In the Artifact console you
accept that agreement and designate an account that can legally process PHI. With
AWS Organizations you can accept the BAA on behalf of all accounts in the
organization, and existing and subsequent member accounts are covered
automatically.

## Compliance programs and who audits them

AWS aligns with a long list of compliance programs in three categories:
certifications and attestations, laws, regulations, and privacy, and alignments
and frameworks. Certifications and attestations are assessed by a third-party,
independent auditor and result in a certification, audit report, or attestation
of compliance. Recognition is enough for the exam: SOC 1, SOC 2, and SOC 3,
ISO 27001, and PCI DSS sit among the global certifications and attestations, and
HIPAA sits under laws and regulations.

<Callout type="note" title="AWS's evidence, not yours">
Artifact hands you AWS's documents. AWS states that customers are responsible for
developing or obtaining the documents that demonstrate the security and
compliance of their own companies, so compliance splits the way security does.
</Callout>

## AWS Organizations puts many accounts under one policy

One account stops being enough quickly. AWS Organizations helps you centrally
manage and govern your environment as you grow: create accounts, group them,
apply policies for governance, and simplify billing with a single payment method
for all of your accounts.

An organization has one management account, the account used to create it. It has
final control over security, infrastructure, and finance policies and pays the
charges of every account in the organization. Every other account is a member
account, and a member account belongs to only one organization at a time.

Organizations also enables an audit capability worth memorizing: you can activate
AWS CloudTrail across accounts, creating a log of all activity in your cloud
environment that member accounts cannot turn off or modify.

## Organizational units group the accounts

Accounts sit under a root and are grouped into organizational units. An OU is a
group of AWS accounts within an organization, and an OU can contain other OUs.
OUs matter because policies attach to them: to apply the same controls to a
subset of accounts, put those accounts in one OU and attach the policy once.
Scenarios use the obvious case, development accounts in one OU and production
accounts in another.

## Service control policies are a ceiling, not a grant

A service control policy offers central control over the maximum available
permissions for the IAM users and IAM roles in your organization.

The sentence to memorize: an SCP never grants permissions. It defines a
permission guardrail, a limit on the actions those users and roles can perform.
Permissions still come from IAM identity-based and resource-based policies, and
effective permissions are the logical intersection of what the SCP allows and
what those policies allow. A user with no IAM permission policy has no access
even when the SCPs allow every service and every action.

Two more rules decide most questions. SCPs affect only member accounts and have
no effect on users or roles in the management account. Inside a member account an
SCP restricts every principal, including that account's root user, so attaching
the AdministratorAccess policy locally does not restore an action the SCP blocks.

That is why an SCP is the answer whenever a restriction must hold
organization-wide. It can limit which services and resources principals reach and
the conditions of their requests, for example the Region. Deny every action
outside an approved Region list, or the actions that stop or delete CloudTrail
logging, and the limit holds for every account in the OU.

<Callout type="tip" title="Read the verb in the option">
If an option says an SCP grants permissions, it is wrong. If a scenario has
permissive SCPs but a user who can do nothing, the missing piece is an IAM
permission policy. If a restriction must survive an account administrator, the
answer is an SCP on the OU.
</Callout>

## AWS Control Tower builds and governs the environment

AWS Control Tower is the setup and governance layer above all of this. AWS calls
it the easiest way to set up and govern a secure, compliant, multi-account AWS
environment based on best practices. It replaces nothing: it orchestrates the
capabilities of other services, including AWS Organizations, AWS Service Catalog,
and AWS IAM Identity Center, to build a landing zone.

A landing zone is a well-architected, multi-account environment based on security
and compliance best practices, the enterprise-wide container that holds the OUs,
accounts, users, and other resources you want subject to compliance regulation.

To keep that environment from drifting, Control Tower applies controls, which AWS
also calls guardrails; the two terms are synonymous. A control is a high-level
rule, expressed in plain language, that provides ongoing governance for your
overall AWS environment, and it applies to an entire OU, so every account in the
OU is affected. Preventive controls disallow actions that lead to policy
violations, detective controls find noncompliant resources and alert you, and
proactive controls scan resources before they are provisioned.

## The account is the natural boundary

Underneath all of it is one idea. AWS accounts are natural boundaries for
permission, security, costs, and workloads, and using a multi-account environment
is a recommended best practice when scaling your cloud environment. So when a
scenario wants the strongest separation between production and a sandbox, the
answer is separate accounts. That does not remove work: accounts share no single
permission set, AWS does not merge them into one security domain, and each still
needs IAM permissions inside it.

## What to take away

Artifact for AWS's compliance documents and agreements such as the BAA, free, and
acceptable organization-wide. Programs such as SOC, ISO 27001, PCI DSS, and
HIPAA, assessed by independent third-party auditors. Organizations for central
management: one management account, many member accounts, OUs, one payment
method. SCPs as a ceiling that grants nothing and skips the management account.
Control Tower for a landing zone governed by controls. And the account as the
boundary all of it rests on.
