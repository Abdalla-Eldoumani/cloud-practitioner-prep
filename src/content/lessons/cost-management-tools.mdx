AWS gives you separate tools for separate questions about money: what will this
cost before I build it, what is it costing now, am I about to overspend, and how
do I attribute costs to teams. The exam tests whether you can match the tool to
the question. This lesson walks through each one and the job it does.

## Estimate before you build: AWS Pricing Calculator

The AWS Pricing Calculator is a free web-based planning tool for creating cost
estimates before you provision anything. You model a solution by choosing
services and configurations, and it returns an estimate with upfront, monthly,
and annual figures. You can group estimates to match an architecture, save a link
to share or revisit, and export to CSV or PDF. You need no prior AWS experience
to use it, which makes it the right tool for planning and budgeting a design you
have not built yet.

<Callout type="key" title="Calculator is for the future, Cost Explorer is for the past">
Use the Pricing Calculator to estimate what a planned workload will cost before
you build it. Use Cost Explorer to analyze what your existing workloads have
already cost. A question about planned or hypothetical spend points to the
Calculator.
</Callout>

## Analyze actual spend: AWS Cost Explorer

AWS Cost Explorer lets you view and analyze your actual costs and usage over
time. It shows up to the last 13 months of data, forecasts likely spend for the
months ahead, and surfaces trends you can drill into with filters and groupings.
You can also get recommendations, such as which Reserved Instances to purchase.
Reach for Cost Explorer when you need to understand where money has gone and
where it is heading. You enable it once per account by opening it in the
console, and once enabled it cannot be disabled again.

## Set alerts and limits: AWS Budgets

AWS Budgets lets you set custom budgets and get alerted when cost or usage crosses
a threshold, including when it is forecasted to exceed the threshold before the
spend actually happens. It supports cost budgets, usage budgets, and budgets that
track Reserved Instance or Savings Plans utilization and coverage. Notifications
can go to email or an Amazon SNS topic, and a budget can even trigger an action.
Budgets is the proactive tool: it warns you before the bill is a surprise.

<Callout type="note" title="Cost Explorer explains, Budgets warns">
Cost Explorer is for analysis after the fact. Budgets is for staying ahead of
spend with thresholds and alerts. A scenario about being notified before going
over a limit points to AWS Budgets.
</Callout>

## The detailed source of truth: AWS Cost and Usage Reports

AWS Cost and Usage Reports (AWS CUR) contains the most comprehensive set of
cost and usage data AWS publishes. It writes line-item detail to an Amazon S3
bucket you own, broken down by hour, day, or month, by product or resource, and
by tags you define. The files are CSV and can grow large, so teams query them
with tools such as Amazon Athena or load them into Amazon Redshift. Use the CUR
when you need the finest-grained billing data for deep analysis or chargeback.
AWS writes the name both ways, so a reference to the Cost and Usage Report in
the singular means this same report.

## The Billing console

The AWS Billing and Cost Management console is the home for billing information.
It shows a spend summary, a breakdown of service costs across the accounts in
your organization, and the credits and discounts applied to your bill. It is also
where you activate cost allocation tags and manage payment details. Think of it
as the dashboard that ties the other tools together.

## Consolidated billing with AWS Organizations

AWS Organizations lets you manage multiple AWS accounts together, and its
consolidated billing feature rolls them up under one management account that pays
the charges for all member accounts. Three benefits matter for the exam:

- One bill. You get a single bill covering all the accounts, with easy tracking
  of charges across them.
- Combined usage for volume discounts. Usage is aggregated across all accounts,
  so the organization reaches volume pricing tiers, Reserved Instance discounts,
  and Savings Plans discounts faster than standalone accounts would. This can
  lower the total charge.
- No additional fee. Consolidated billing itself costs nothing extra.

<Callout type="tip" title="Why consolidate billing">
The tested benefit is the combined-usage volume discount. Many small accounts
that each stay in a low pricing tier pay more than the same usage aggregated
under one organization, because the combined total reaches cheaper tiers.
</Callout>

## Attribute costs: cost allocation tags

A tag is a key-value label you attach to a resource. Cost allocation tags let you
organize and track costs at a detailed level, for example by cost center,
application, or owner. AWS provides two kinds: AWS-generated tags, which AWS
creates and applies for you, and user-defined tags, which you create. Both must
be activated in the Billing console before they appear in Cost Explorer or on a
cost allocation report. Once active, your costs show up grouped by tag, which is
how organizations split a shared bill across teams.

## What to take away

Match the tool to the question. The Pricing Calculator estimates a design before
you build it. Cost Explorer analyzes and forecasts actual spend. Budgets alerts
you before you cross a threshold. Cost and Usage Reports are the most detailed
billing data, delivered to S3. The Billing console is the dashboard.
Consolidated billing under AWS Organizations gives one bill and combined-usage
volume discounts at no extra fee, and cost allocation tags let you attribute
spend to teams.
